# Summary: Economic Model Predictive Control (eMPC)

## 1. Introduction to Economic MPC
* Standard MPC focuses on stabilizing a system using a quadratic cost function (tracking). However, real-world modern control problems require optimizing complex performance metrics such as economic profits, energy consumption, and material use.
* **Economic Cost Function:** Instead of a tracking penalty, eMPC utilizes an economic stage cost $\ell(x, u)$. This function represents direct economic losses or costs. 
* To ensure the optimization problem is well-defined and does not diverge to negative infinity, $\ell(x, u)$ must be a continuous and **lower-bounded** function.

## 2. The Two-Stage (Cascaded) Approach
A traditional, simpler way to handle economic objectives is to split the problem into two distinct stages:
1.  **Offline Steady-State Optimization:** Find the most profitable working point $(x_s, u_s)$ by solving a static optimization problem:
    $$\min_{x_s, u_s} \ell(x_s, u_s)$$
    subject to the physical equilibrium constraint $x_s = f(x_s, u_s)$.
2.  **Online Tracking (Standard MPC):** Use a standard MPC with a quadratic cost function $g(x_k - x_s, u_k - u_s)$ to track the calculated steady-state target.
* **Drawback:** While mathematically simpler to analyze, this cascaded approach generally yields a **suboptimal** dynamic trajectory, wasting resources during the transition.

## 3. Direct Economic MPC (eMPC)
* To eliminate the inefficiency of the two-stage approach, Economic MPC integrates the loss function directly into the dynamic optimization problem:
  $$\min_{\mathbf{u}} \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \ell(x_k, u_k)$$
  subject to system dynamics, operational constraints, and a critical terminal constraint $x_K = x_s$.
* **Computational Challenge:** eMPC is significantly harder to solve in real-time. Unlike tracking MPC where the cost is perfectly zero at the equilibrium, eMPC problems are often **non-convex**. There may exist transient operating points $(x, u)$ that are economically cheaper than the steady-state itself ($\ell(x, u) < \ell(x_s, u_s)$).

## 4. The Myopia Problem and Terminal Constraints
* If the horizon $K$ is too short, eMPC can act myopically (e.g., shutting down a furnace completely to save immediate fuel costs, ignoring the catastrophic long-term cost of a frozen furnace). 
* To prevent the emergence of these "cheap now, expensive later" unstable trajectories, eMPC **must** enforce the terminal constraint **$x_K = x_s$**. This guarantees that the system remains in a recoverable, operational state at the end of the horizon.

## 5. Breakdown of Lyapunov Stability
* In standard tracking MPC, the optimal cost function $W(x)$ serves as a Lyapunov function because it strictly decreases over time.
* In eMPC, the standard Lyapunov proof fails because the stage cost $\ell(x, u)$ is not necessarily minimized at the steady-state. The inequality:
  $$W(x_{i+1}) \le W(x_i) + \ell(x_s, u_s) - \ell(x_i, u_0^\ast(x_i))$$
  shows that if a transient state is cheaper than the steady-state, the term $[\ell(x_s, u_s) - \ell(x_i, u_0^\ast(x_i))]$ becomes positive. 
* Consequently, $W(x_{i+1})$ might be larger than $W(x_i)$. Since the "energy" is no longer strictly decreasing, $W(x)$ loses its property as a standard Lyapunov function, making stability proofs for eMPC highly complex.

## 6. Theorem 3: Long-Term Performance Guarantee
* Because eMPC is not strictly driven to a single equilibrium, it often generates **semi-periodic trajectories** (dynamic fluctuations that save more energy/money than staying completely still).
* **Theorem 3** mathematically guarantees that for a sufficiently large horizon, the dynamic trajectory generated by eMPC is on average *at least* as economically efficient as simply sitting at the optimal steady-state:
  $$\limsup_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \ell(x_k, u_k) \le \ell(x_s, u_s)$$
* **Proof Intuition:** By summing the modified Lyapunov inequality over $N$ steps, a telescoping sum is formed ($\frac{W(x_N) - W(x_0)}{N}$). As $N \to \infty$, since $W(x)$ is lower-bounded, this fractional term vanishes to zero, proving the average performance bound.
